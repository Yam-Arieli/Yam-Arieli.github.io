01 · The gap

A single snapshot of a moving system

Cascades of the immune system are important to understand: one signaling state triggers the next in a defined order. But most of our single-cell RNA-sequencing data comes from a single time snapshot, because the sequencing process kills the cell as it reads it. There's no way to sequence the same cell twice and watch it move through the cascade, every dataset is one frozen frame, with no time axis to read directly off the data.

02 · The idea

Infer direction without a time axis

A methodology to infer how systemic programs unfold directionally over time from a single static snapshot, bypassing the need for time-course experiments or domain-specific priors altogether.

03 · Implementation

An attention-based MIL model

An interpretable, attention-based Multiple Instance Learning model recovers underlying systemic programs, extracting feature signatures via gradient-field integration and matrix normalization to isolate signal couplings from global artifacts. Probabilistic gating mechanisms over robust median aggregations then separate true signal cascades from stochastic noise.

04 · Results

83-100% accuracy

The framework validated at 83-100% accuracy across diverse, real-world datasets.

Algorithmic Robotics

Configuration spaces, forward and inverse kinematics, statics, and the dynamics of robotic arms: the first eight chapters of Lynch, Kevin M. and Frank C. Park, Modern Robotics, Cambridge University Press, 2017. Describing a system by the space of states it can occupy, and reading motion as a field over that space, is the same machinery my thesis reuses to recover direction by integrating a gradient field.

Algorithms For Big Data

Three computational models for data too large to hold: streaming algorithms that work in less memory than the input, sublinear-time algorithms that answer without reading all of it, and distributed and local computation. Both halves of my work sit here: ETLs at the PMO that had to process updates incrementally rather than reloading everything, and a thesis working over millions of cells with thousands of genes each.

Sandbox

Two vessels, one snapshot

Redness and squareness are two pseudo-effects a cytokine can leave on a cell. Redness stands for an immune response; squareness for a controlled release of one — a squared cell goes on to secrete cytokine Y itself. So X, which squares cells, drives Y downstream. Y reddens cells and squares them slightly too, but that slight squaring is incidental noise from a messy perturbation, not a signal being passed on. The cascade X → Y is real; Y → X is not.

All we get to observe is the cells — their redness and squareness, standing in for a gene-expression profile — and which cytokine we chose to inject into each vessel. The cytokines themselves are invisible: we never watch one bind, and we never see the Y a cell secretes. That hidden Y is precisely what we are trying to prove exists. So the plot reduces each vessel to one (squareness, redness) vector and drops each onto the other’s axis; the gap between those two projections is the cascade, recovered from the cells alone.
